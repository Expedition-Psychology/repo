The Challenge Point Framework

The session that makes you look best isn't always the session that helps you learn most.

Dr Fin Haley

Clinical Psychologist & Rock Climbing Instructor

There are two easy ways to organise a climbing session. Stay on familiar problems and collect successes, or throw yourself at something so difficult that every attempt ends in the same place.

The first feels reassuring. The second feels committed. Neither necessarily gives you the most useful practice.

Learning asks a different question: is the challenge giving you information you can actually use?

The challenge point framework offers a way to think about that balance. In this series, we're talking about learning movement and decision-making skills, rather than prescribing strength or endurance training.

1Difficulty depends on the person doing the task

The framework, developed by Mark Guadagnoli and Timothy Lee, distinguishes between a task's nominal difficulty and its functional difficulty: how demanding it is for a particular learner under particular conditions.

A straightforward footwork drill for an experienced climber might require a beginner's full attention. The same exercise may become harder when the footholds change, when instructions are removed, or when the movement must be chosen rather than copied.

Difficulty isn't just the grade written beside the climb. It's the combination of the task, the person, and the way practice is arranged.

That gives you more ways to adjust training than simply choosing something harder.

2Make mistakes that teach you something

An error is useful when it helps you identify what to change. You placed the foot inaccurately. You moved before establishing balance. You chose a sequence that left no useful position for the next move.

When a task is far beyond your current skill, the information may be less useful. Everything happens too quickly, several things go wrong at once, and you're left with "I can't do it" rather than a specific adjustment.

At the other extreme, an exercise can become so predictable that it no longer asks anything new of you. Easy practice still has a place for warming up, consolidating skills or rebuilding confidence; it just serves a different purpose.

The challenge point is about matching the amount of usable information to the learner, not maximising failure.

3Use success rates as clues, not laws

For a low-risk technical drill, succeeding roughly three times in four can be a reasonable coaching starting point. There are enough successes to recognise what works, with enough errors to investigate.

But the often-quoted 70-80% range is not a universal prescription from the challenge point framework. The appropriate balance depends on the skill, experience, practice conditions and consequences of error.

Ask three questions instead. Am I having to pay attention? Can I identify what an error tells me? Am I finding changes that sometimes help?

Repeated failure with no useful feedback suggests the task may need simplifying. Repeated success without meaningful attention suggests it may be ready to develop.

Where errors could cause harm, safety requirements take priority. You don't engineer a failure rate into belaying, navigation decisions with serious consequences, or other safety-critical tasks.

4Adjust one useful demand

Take foot placement on an appropriate, low-risk practice wall. Begin with clear targets and a manageable sequence. Once placement becomes reliable, change one feature: a less obvious target, a different movement direction, or a sequence that requires choosing between options.

Keep the focus on the skill you're trying to learn. Making the handholds much harder may turn a footwork task into a strength test.

After a few attempts, review the feedback and adjust. Then revisit the skill another day and on a different suitable route. Performing it once at the end of a familiar drill is not the same as being able to use it later.

Productive training isn't about proving how hard you can make a session. It's about choosing a difficulty from which you can learn.
