The Importance of Variability

The goal isn't to become excellent at one drill. It's to use the skill when the situation changes.

Dr Fin Haley

Clinical Psychologist & Rock Climbing Instructor

You've practised a heel hook until it feels reliable. Same hold, same angle, same sequence. You know exactly where to put your foot and when to pull.

Then a different route asks for something that looks similar, and the movement doesn't work.

That doesn't mean the practice was pointless. It means the new situation is asking for something your original repetitions may not have covered: adaptation.

Variability is about creating opportunities to learn that adaptation, while keeping practice relevant to the skill you want to develop.

1Vary the skills you bring to the session

At the broader level, variability means developing a range of abilities rather than practising only what already suits you.

A climber who loves steep, powerful movement may spend most sessions there. That can be enjoyable and productive, but it leaves other demands less familiar: delicate balance, route reading, movement on smaller footholds.

The aim isn't to become equally good at everything. It is to identify the range your own goals require.

Planning a long mountain route may call for different skills from preparing for a short indoor bouldering competition. Choose variety that serves the activity, rather than collecting weaknesses simply to have more things to fix.

2Vary the conditions around one skill

At the smaller level, variability means changing how the same skill is used.

With foot placement, you might work across different suitable foothold shapes, movement directions or wall angles. With passing, you might vary distance and the position of a teammate. With pacing, you might practise responding to changes in terrain within an appropriate training plan.

The common question is, "What does this situation require?"

Rather than memorising one answer, you are practising selecting and adapting an answer. A useful variation preserves the skill's purpose while changing something the performer needs to notice.

That is different from making an exercise random for the sake of it.

3Expect practice to feel less tidy

When the same solution works repeatedly, performance can become fluent within a session. Changing the conditions often interrupts that fluency.

This can feel like regression. Sometimes it is simply the cost of having to work the problem out again.

Research on blocked and interleaved motor practice shows that the schedule which produces smoother practice is not always the one that produces better retention or transfer. The effects are task-dependent; variety is not automatically superior for every learner or every stage.

A beginner may benefit from enough repetition to understand the basic task before several demands change. A more experienced performer may need variation to keep the practice informative.

The challenge point still matters. Variety should create useful decisions, not so much confusion that nothing can be learned.

4Change one thing, then test the transfer

Choose a skill and start with a version you understand. Once you have a workable solution, alter one relevant feature.

For example, after practising accurate foot placement on obvious footholds, use a different appropriate route where the footholds face another direction. Keep the climbing manageable enough that you can still work on the intended skill.

Compare what stayed useful and what needed adjusting. Did your old solution fit? What information helped you choose a new one?

Later, try a suitable route you haven't used in the drill. This is the important test: does the skill appear when you aren't being explicitly walked through it?

Useful variety can also make training more interesting, but novelty alone is not the target. Nor should variation involve removing safety equipment, bypassing supervision or adding dangerous consequences.

You are developing a flexible skill, not trying to prove that you can cope with anything.
