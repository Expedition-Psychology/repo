Summer Alpine Climbing

Route Risk Assessment

A structured planning tool for rating objective hazards, checking your readiness, and deciding — deliberately — before you commit to a route.

Before you start

Fill this out at the planning stage

This worksheet is designed to be completed while you are still rested and rational — before the mountain, the weather, and your own ego start negotiating with you. It walks you through the objective hazards you can't control, the readiness factors you can, the decision traps that quietly seed accidents, and the turnaround criteria you commit to now.

It structures your judgement; it does not replace it. Most controls are dropdowns, so the sheet fills in a couple of minutes. Free text is reserved for the few places where judgement genuinely needs words.

How it works

1

In Part A you rate each objective hazard with a likelihood × consequence matrix. Each hazard gets an automatic risk level — Low, Moderate, High, or Extreme.

2

Parts B–D check your readiness, surface your decision traps, and lock in your turnaround criteria.

3

Part E reads it all back. The worst single hazard drives the result — never an average — and readiness and decision-trap flags can raise it further or stop the trip outright.

Your privacy. Everything you enter stays on this device, in your browser only — it is never uploaded or shared. Turn saving off with the switch at the top, and use Clear on a shared computer when you finish.

Read this before you use it. On serious alpine ground the consequence is rarely below Moderate — a slip in the wrong place is fatal wherever you are on the route. Most honest ratings land amber or worse, and that's correct: alpine climbing is a high-consequence activity. The matrix isn't there to reassure you. It's there to make you rate each hazard deliberately, compare them, and see which one is driving the decision. An Extreme cell is a stop sign, not a number to be balanced against a good forecast elsewhere.

Within your control

Readiness

These are entirely yours. Be honest — self-assessment is where accidents are quietly seeded. Any single Insufficient means you are not ready for this route today.

Technical competence vs the grade The hardest move on route, climbed recently in the mountains, with margin.
Core alpine skills, current Crevasse rescue, ice-axe arrest, retreat/abseiling, belay building. When did you last actually practise crevasse rescue?
Fitness & current condition Endurance for the full day; any illness, injury or accumulated fatigue.
Acclimatisation for the altitude
Equipment & emergency kit Appropriate to route and conditions, with redundancy; bivvy, first aid, spare headtorch, repair kit.
Navigation, route knowledge & conditions intel Topo/GPS, and recent reports — hut warden, recent ascents.
Party competence & pace Everyone up to the route; planned to the slowest member, not the fittest.
Comms & emergency plan Plan lodged with someone; comms and rescue cover in place.
C
Usually invisible

Decision traps

The objective hazards rarely kill on their own — decisions do. These biases are strongest exactly when you're tired, invested and near the goal. Flag them now, rested and honest, so you'll recognise them later.

Summit fever Is wanting it distorting how you read the conditions?
Sunk cost Can you walk away from effort already spent? "We've come this far" is not a reason.
External deadlines Flights, work, a closing window pushing you up the hill.
Groupthink / expert halo Continuing because no one wants to call it, or deferring uncritically to the "strongest" climber.
Complacency Treating a serious route as routine because it's familiar or you've succeeded before.
Fear mis-read Ignoring a legitimate gut warning, or letting anxiety override sound judgement.
Fatigue & judgement How will you notice when your thinking has gone downhill?
Ego / validation Whose approval are you climbing for?
