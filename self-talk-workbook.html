Performance · Working With Self-Talk

The Self Talk Workbook

The aim isn't to tell yourself everything will go well. It's to make your inner commentary visible, then choose words that serve the task instead of adding pressure. Works for any sport.

Before you start

Helpful matters more than positive

Picture a training partner saying the things your commentary says mid-attempt: "You're going to mess this up, everyone can see you're struggling." You'd question whether they were helping. Our own version becomes so familiar we stop noticing it.

Thoughts shape emotion, attention and behaviour, but they aren't the only cause of difficulty: fatigue, pain, conditions and real demands matter too. So the useful question isn't whether a thought is positive enough. It's what happens when you get caught up in it, and what you could say instead that gives you a workable next action.

Step one · Notice the script

Catch your recurring pressure thoughts

Write down the negative automatic thoughts that show up under pressure, and when they tend to arrive: before starting, after an error, near the finish, or when someone's watching. Predictable moments give you something to prepare for.

{{ t.n }} Pressure thought
Notice what each thought does to you. "I mustn't slip" pulls attention toward the mistake. "Everyone thinks I'm useless" asks you to solve an unknowable social problem mid-performance. You're not judging whether a thought is nice, you're spotting the ones that get in the way.
Step two · Workable alternatives

Choose words you can believe and use

For each thought, decide what job the response needs to do, write a believable alternative, and boil it down to one short cue you can use mid-performance. None of these predicts success, each just gives you a more workable relationship with the moment.

Instructional

Directs attention to an action. "Place the foot."

Motivational

Supports effort or commitment. "Drive through."

Compassionate

Helps you recover without self-attack. "That was hard. What next?"

What job should the response do?
Avoid phrases that feel obviously false, you don't have to convince yourself the challenge is easy. "Don't make another mistake" can become "choose the target"; "that was pathetic" can become "that was difficult, what can I learn before the next one?" Keep the long reflection for before or after; in the middle of a hard moment, one short cue is plenty.
Step three · Don't argue, redirect

Make room for the thought, then act

Trying to delete every negative thought becomes its own distraction, you notice doubt, argue with it, check whether the argument worked, and lose the activity. Instead, name the thought and point yourself at the cue. The thought can still be there; you're just making room for a useful action alongside it.

Keep cues appropriate to the context

A cue should never talk you past a real safety concern. Note where "pause and check" is the genuinely helpful instruction, so a motivational cue doesn't override your judgement.
Step four · Rehearse in training

Knowing a good phrase isn't enough

Self-talk works through deliberate practice, not good intentions. Use each response where it belongs, in ordinary training, then review the effect and adapt the wording. A phrase that helps a teammate may not help you.

Afterwards, review the effect
The goal is a way of speaking to yourself that's honest about difficulty and still useful within it. You can care deeply about improving without making yourself the opponent. Timing matters as much as wording, and you don't have to remove a thought before choosing an action.
