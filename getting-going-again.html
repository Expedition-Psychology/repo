Low Mood · Activation Before Motivation

Getting Going Again

A behavioural activation workbook — a practical way to rebuild routine, achievement and enjoyment when low mood has pulled you away from the things you usually do.

About This Workbook

What is behavioural activation?

Behavioural activation is a straightforward approach to low mood, recommended by NICE. It works from the outside in: rather than waiting to feel motivated before you act, you plan small activities first and let motivation follow. Activation before motivation.

When mood drops, it is common to withdraw — to stop seeing people, to put off the washing or the bills, to spend longer in bed. Each of those choices makes sense in the moment. Over time, though, they remove the very things that lift mood: structure, a sense of achievement, connection with others, and enjoyment. The withdrawal quietly keeps the low mood going.

Work through the tabs in order, at your own pace. Only move on when the step you are on feels steady, and give it time — a few weeks of small, regular activity does more than one ambitious week.

Routine
Day-to-day activities that keep life comfortable and ordered.
Washing, cooking, housework, a regular bedtime.
Necessary
Things that carry real consequences the longer they are left.
Paying bills, appointments, admin, job applications.
Pleasurable
Activities that bring enjoyment, achievement or connection.
Seeing friends, hobbies, gardening, sport, learning.

Think of a three-legged stool: all three legs need to be roughly the same height for it to hold steady. A week made only of routine and necessary activities is as unbalanced as a week with none of them.

The cycle feeds itself
Each thing left undone confirms the belief that there is no point starting, and lowers mood a little further — which makes the next activity harder still. Behavioural activation breaks in at the behaviour, because that is the part you can change directly.
Step One

Keeping a baseline diary

Start by recording what you already do, exactly as things are — no changes yet. A week of honest notes shows where your time goes, what you have withdrawn from, and how different activities affect your mood.


Write activities down as you do them

Anything counts — work, chores, meals, a phone call. Note the quieter stretches too: time in bed, hours on the sofa.


Record it as it is, not as you would like it

This is information, not a test. There are no wrong entries.


Rate your mood before and after

A word, or a number out of 100. You will start to see which activities lift mood and which flatten it.

Step Two

Identifying and ranking activities

List what you are not doing: things you used to enjoy, things you have been putting off, things you never find time for — and things you have always wanted to try. Your baseline diary will help you spot the gaps.

For each one, choose a category and how difficult it feels right now. Your ranked plan builds itself below as you go.

Add an activity

“Easiest” does not mean easy. When mood is low nothing is easy — it simply means the most manageable place to start.
Nothing listed yet. Start with one small thing you have stopped doing.
Step Three · Built From Your List

Your ranked plan

Begin with the easiest level and work down the list. Move up a level only when that one feels steady. Aim for at least one routine, one necessary and one pleasurable activity at each level, so whichever level you are working at, your week stays balanced.

If nothing feels easy enough

  • Break a large task into smaller pieces — “dust the living room for twenty minutes” rather than “clean the house”.
  • Aim for ten minutes of an activity rather than finishing it.
  • No activity is too small. Listening to one voicemail counts.
  • Ask a friend or family member to do it alongside you.
Step Four

Scheduling activities

A planned activity is far more likely to happen than an intended one. Put your chosen activities into the week ahead, holding three conditions in mind.

1

Graded

Start with the easiest level from your ranked plan. Move up only when that level feels steady.

2

Balanced

Mix routine, necessary and pleasurable across the week. If your days are full of necessary tasks, use the diary to decide what can wait so something enjoyable fits.

3

Specific

Name the what, when, where and who. “Clean the worktops at 4:30pm in the kitchen, by myself” is a plan; “tidy up” is a wish.

Do not fill the week. Two or three small activities is a good first week — you can add more as your mood lifts.

Step Five

Doing them, and watching your mood

The last step is to carry out what you planned and record how it went. Rating your mood before and after each activity is what makes the effect visible. Some activities lift mood immediately; others take a few repetitions before they do.

One guiding line: complete activities in spite of how you feel, not because of how you feel.

·

Be flexible

Life gets in the way. An activity that did not happen is postponed, not failed — move it to a day when it feels achievable.

·

Review weekly

Look back at the week: what went well, what got in the way. Then add a little more, at your own pace.

Break it into smaller chunks and do one.
No energy or motivation
Be time-specific rather than task-specific: twenty minutes, not “finish it”.
Not enough time
Prioritise, and protect the balance — something pleasurable stays in.
No improvement in mood yet
Keep going. Mood usually follows activity, and it follows late.
Holding On To It

Maintaining progress

Progress is easier to keep than to rebuild. Come back to these questions when things are going well, so you know what to reach for when they dip.

This workbook is a self-help tool, not a substitute for care. If your mood stays low, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please speak with your GP or a mental health professional.

Motivation rarely arrives first. Start small, and let it follow.

Behavioural activation is recommended by NICE for the treatment of depression (NG222). Further reading: A Recovery Programme for Depression, Lovell & Richards; Overcoming Depression and Low Mood, Chris Williams.
