The Importance of Gamification

Useful practice can be playful. The trick is to make the game reward the skill you actually want to learn.

Ask someone to repeat a footwork drill for twenty minutes and you may lose their attention. Give them a clear challenge, a way to notice progress, and a partner who is interested, and the same movements can feel very different.

That is the appeal of gamification: bringing elements of games into an activity that isn't formally a game.

Levels, shared challenges, points and small constraints can give practice a purpose that is easy to engage with. But adding a scoreboard doesn't automatically make training more useful.

The design of the game matters.

1. Begin with the learning, not the scoring

Before inventing the rules, decide what you want to develop.

Perhaps it is deliberate foot placement, recognising a useful rest, communicating clearly, or trying more than one solution to a movement problem.

Then ask what behaviour the game will actually reward. A speed challenge may encourage rushing. A points system based only on finished routes may encourage choosing easy routes or avoiding experiments.

Neither is automatically wrong, but either could work against a technical learning goal.

Good game design makes the useful behaviour the thing worth doing. The score is feedback, not the purpose of the session.

2. Create a challenge with room for choice

Imagine a low-level foot-placement game on an appropriate practice wall. The aim is to place the foot deliberately on a chosen target. Give yourself credit for an accurate placement, then notice whether you can use it effectively in the next move.

Start at a manageable level. As the task becomes reliable, choose a meaningful variation: a different direction or another suitable sequence.

The criteria should serve the skill. Silence, for example, can be a useful cue in some footwork exercises, but a quiet contact isn't automatically an effective one. Don't let the scoring replace judgement.

Allow choice between suitable versions. A person who understands the aim and helps shape the challenge has more ownership than someone simply obeying a rule.

3. Use other people for connection, not just comparison

Competition can be enjoyable, but it isn't the only social ingredient available.

Two climbers could work together to find three workable solutions to a sequence. A team could earn a shared score for clear communication. Partners could alternate between practising and giving one specific piece of feedback.

These games make someone else's progress useful rather than threatening.

Research on game design suggests that different features can support different psychological needs. It does not show that every game, badge or leaderboard improves motivation, or that findings from digital tasks automatically transfer to climbing.

In practice, ask whether the game helps people feel capable, involved and connected. A leaderboard that repeatedly embarrasses one participant is not made helpful by calling it fun.

4. Keep mistakes informative and the rules flexible

A level system can create a satisfying sense of progress. But sending someone back to the beginning after every mistake may produce frustration or cautious behaviour rather than useful exploration.

Sometimes a reset adds a light challenge. Sometimes it teaches people not to risk learning anything new. Use the response of the person in front of you as feedback.

Consider rewarding a thoughtful adjustment, a new solution or a useful observation alongside successful execution. End with a short review: what did the game help you practise, and did that skill appear when the game stopped?

Safety rules are never points to trade away. No game should reward bypassing checks, unnecessary risk, humiliating forfeits or pushing through pain.

Play works best when it supports the reasons you came to the sport in the first place: curiosity, movement, connection and the pleasure of getting better at something.
