Facing It, Step by Step

A graded exposure workbook, from Expedition Psychology — turning towards fear, one rung at a time.

About this workbook

How facing fear, gently, sets you free

Fear is a normal part of being human — but when it starts making our choices for us, our world can quietly shrink. This workbook is built on one of the most reliable tools in psychology for loosening fear's grip: graded exposure. The idea is simple, though not always easy — we turn towards what frightens us, in small, manageable steps, and stay long enough to learn that we can cope.

Below is the short version of how and why it works. Take it in at your own pace — understanding the why makes the doing far easier.

How anxiety works

Anxiety is your body's protective alarm — the fight-or-flight response doing its job. It can feel intense, but it isn't dangerous, and it can't keep climbing forever. Left to run its course, it rises, peaks, and comes back down every single time.

The avoidance trap

When something frightens us, avoiding it brings instant relief — and that relief is exactly why avoidance is so sticky. But it's a short-term fix with a long-term cost: every time we avoid, we teach our brain the situation really was dangerous, the fear stays strong, and bit by bit our world gets smaller.

Two paths, two outcomes

How exposure helps

Exposure is the opposite move: gently, deliberately turning towards what we fear, in steps we can manage, and staying long enough to let the anxiety settle. Do that and something changes — the body learns the alarm can be ridden out, and that the feared outcome usually doesn't come. The fear loses its grip. This is the engine of everything that follows.

The four rules of exposure

Rule one

Graded

Build a ladder and climb one rung at a time. Choose steps that take you just outside your comfort zone — enough to feel the anxiety, but not so much that you tip into panic. If a rung feels overwhelming, break it into a smaller one.

Rule two

Prolonged

Stay with it long enough for the anxiety to rise and then ease. Rather than watching for a set number, let the moment run until you feel genuinely calmer and more settled. Leaving while you're still highly anxious teaches your brain the situation really was a threat.

Rule three

Repeated

Practise often. Repeat a rung until it reliably feels easier before you move up.

Rule four

Without safety behaviours

Face it without the crutches, props, or distractions you lean on to feel safe. They feel helpful, but they stop you learning that you're okay without them.

How to use this workbook

1

Build your ladder. List the situations you've been avoiding and rate how anxious each one feels, from calm to your most anxious. They'll sort themselves into a hierarchy you can climb.

2

Pack your strategies. Choose a few ways to stay with the anxiety rather than escape it — you'll carry these up the ladder with you.

3

Plan, do, and reflect. Work one rung at a time. Log each attempt, watch the anxiety rise and fall, and note what you learned.

4

Watch it soften. Re-rate your rungs and track your peaks over time. The proof is in the trend — what once felt like an 80 starts to feel like a 40.

A note on looking after yourself. This is a self-help tool, not a substitute for therapy. It works best for everyday fears and avoidance. If your anxiety is severe or overwhelming, feels unmanageable, or is linked to trauma, please work through it alongside a qualified professional — you deserve that support, and exposure is far safer and more effective with the right guidance. There's no rush, and going gently is not going backwards.

01
The anxiety hierarchy

Build your ladder

List the situations you've been avoiding, and rate how anxious each one feels right now — from 0 (completely calm) to 100 (the most anxious you can imagine). As you rate them, they arrange themselves into a ladder: the hardest rungs at the top, the gentlest at the bottom, ready to climb.

Aim for a good spread of ratings — a few gentle rungs low down for early wins, not a ladder that jumps straight to the top. When you're choosing which rung to work on next, pick one that sits just beyond your comfort zone: challenging enough to feel it, but not so much that it tips into panic. Come back and re-rate any rung as it starts to feel easier — watching the numbers fall is the whole point.
02
Your coping toolkit

Pack your strategies

Before you face anything, decide how you'll stay with the anxiety. These are ways to stay engaged with the feeling until it settles — not ways to dampen, distract from, or escape it. Tick the ones you'll lean on, and add your own.

Which strategies will help you stay present and ride the wave?
Add any strategies of your own.
Coping vs. escaping — the crucial difference. Genuine coping keeps you in the moment: breathing, kind and realistic self-talk, accepting the feeling, staying present. Safety behaviours and distraction pull you out of it — scrolling your phone, gripping a lucky object, seeking reassurance, or mentally checking out. They feel helpful, but they quietly stop the learning from happening, which is exactly what rule four is about. If a strategy is helping you stay and feel the anxiety soften, it's coping. If it's helping you not really be there, it's a safety behaviour to notice and, over time, let go.

Your exposure log

This is where you plan each exposure, carry it out, and reflect on what happened. Each attempt draws its own anxiety graph — the "it came, and it went" picture. Log a new one every time you practise, and build up a running record.
