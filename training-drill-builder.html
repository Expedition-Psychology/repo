Training · The Drill Builder

Build a drill that actually teaches

The session that makes you look best isn't always the one that helps you learn most. Take one skill and one drill, and shape them so every attempt gives you information you can use. Works for any sport.

Before you start

What makes a drill worth doing?

It's easy to repeat what you're already good at, or to batter away at something so hard every rep ends the same way. Neither reliably teaches you much. A good drill lives in between: hard enough to make you think, forgiving enough that your mistakes point to a fix.

You'll shape one drill in seven short moves, each drawn from a principle of effective practice: the right difficulty, a ladder of levels, intentional spacing, variety, and a bit of play. By the end you'll have a drill you can run and a way to tell whether it's working.

Step one

What skill are you trying to improve?

Pick one movement or decision-making skill. The whole drill works best aimed at a single, clear thing rather than a whole session at once.

Why this matters

The same task isn't equally hard for everyone. A drill is only "right" relative to the athlete doing it, so the build starts with you and this one skill. Naming where you stand and what the skill is ultimately for keeps every later choice pointed at learning you can use.
Step two · The drill

What drill could help you improve it?

Describe a simple, repeatable exercise that puts the important part of the skill front and centre. Keep it about this skill, so competing demands don't quietly turn it into a different test.

Why this matters

Deciding what a good rep looks like now gives you something to measure against on every attempt. Without it, "did that work?" becomes a feeling rather than information, and you can't tell whether the drill is set at the right level.
Step three · The right difficulty

Make it hard enough to get it right most of the time

Aim for a drill you get right roughly seven or eight times out of ten. That's enough success to know what works, with enough misses to learn from. Too easy and it teaches nothing; too hard and everything goes wrong at once.

The 70–80% figure is a handy rule of thumb, not a law; the right balance depends on the skill, your experience and the consequences of a mistake. Use three questions as your real guide: am I having to pay attention, can I tell what each miss is telling me, and am I finding changes that sometimes help? Where a mistake could cause harm, safety beats any target.
Step four · Levels

What levels above this raise the complexity?

Plan the steps up before you need them, so progress is a decision rather than a guess. Add one main demand per level, and be able to say what each level asks that the one before it didn't.

Change one thing at a time, so when performance dips you know why. Nailing it once isn't the same as owning it, so move up on a pattern of good reps plus the ability to recognise and fix a miss, not a single lucky success. It's fine to drop back a level when a session is off; that's using feedback, not going backwards.
Step five · Reps and breaks

Make the reps and breaks intentional

Decide up front how long you'll work before you stop, and what the break is for. Spacing the practice, rather than grinding until it falls apart, is what makes the skill stick and keeps each rep deliberate.

A time limit organises your attention; the break and the return are what distribute the learning. Use the break to recover, notice what happened, and pick one change worth trying, then let rest be rest. These numbers are examples, not doses, so fit them around your sport and recovery. Describe what you saw ("my touch went square") rather than judging yourself ("I'm useless at this").
Step six · Variety

Practise it in different contexts

The goal isn't to be great at one drill; it's to use the skill when the situation changes. List ways to change the conditions around the drill so you practise adapting the skill, not memorising one answer. Change one feature at a time.

Conditions you'll vary
Expect varied practice to feel less tidy; the version that feels smoother isn't always the one that helps it transfer to competition. A beginner may need plenty of repetition to grasp the basics first, while a more experienced athlete usually needs variety to keep practice informative. Choose variety that serves your goal, not novelty for its own sake, and never by removing safety.
Step seven · Make it fun

Turn the drill into a game

Attention is easier to hold with a clear challenge and a way to see progress. Pick a rule or two to build in, then write your own version. The trick is to make the game reward the good rep, not just finishing.

What you score shapes what you practise: a race against the clock can reward rushing, and points only for finishing can reward playing it safe. Let the score be feedback, not the point. A reset after a miss adds a nice challenge for some and teaches caution in others, so use how it feels as your guide. Finish with a quick review: what did the game help me practise, and did it show up when the game stopped?
