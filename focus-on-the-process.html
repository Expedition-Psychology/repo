Focus on the Process

Setting goals you can actually control — a short, practical tool from Expedition Psychology.

About this worksheet

Why the process matters more than the outcome

When something matters to us, it is natural to fix our attention on the result — passing the assessment, winning the place, hitting the grade, landing the job. Those outcome goals are genuinely useful: they give us direction and can be strongly motivating. But they are only ever partly in our control. Conditions, other people, timing, and luck all have a say.

Your process goals are different. They are the actions within your control — what you do, where you put your attention, and how you respond when things get hard. Anchoring on the process is one of the most reliable ways to build confidence, steady your nerves, and keep your focus where it is actually useful, especially under pressure.

Over-focusing on the outcome tends to work against you. Because the result is not fully yours to command, fixating on it quietly raises anxiety and can knock your confidence when progress feels slow. The practical stance is simple: use the outcome to point the way, then live in the process.

This worksheet gives you two ways to do that — one for a single session, one for a longer-term goal. There are no wrong answers here. Approach it with curiosity and a little kindness towards yourself.

01
Short session

Set the scene & your process goal

The one controllable thing you'll commit to doing over the next hour or day — regardless of how it turns out.

Keep it specific and yours. A process goal might look like:

  • Keep my breathing slow and steady.
  • Commit fully to each attempt.
  • Speak at a measured pace.
  • Refocus after any mistake within one breath.
Set the one process goal that matters most for this session. Just one. A single, clear focus is easier to hold under pressure — too many can be overwhelming and pull your attention in different directions.

Longer-term goal

Name the outcome — and why it matters

Outcome goals give direction and can be strongly motivating. Name yours, then keep it in its place.

Outcome goals — passing the diploma, winning the race, hitting the grade — are useful for direction and motivation. But they're only partly in your control, and fixing on them can raise anxiety when progress is slow. So: name the outcome, then translate it into the process behaviours you can control day to day — and keep your attention there.
