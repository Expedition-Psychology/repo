The Importance of Preparation

Preparation isn't about predicting everything. It's about improving what you can and practising how you will respond to the rest.

Dr Fin Haley

Clinical Psychologist & Rock Climbing Instructor

You've trained for the event. You feel strong, the equipment is packed, and the plan looks straightforward.

Then the start is delayed. The venue is noisier than expected. The first section doesn't suit your strengths. Suddenly, a day you felt ready for begins to feel unfamiliar.

This is where preparation needs to go beyond the ideal version of an event.

Physical readiness matters. So does having a way to think, decide and adapt when reality refuses to follow the plan.

1Prepare for demands, not just dates

A date in the diary tells you when you need to be ready. It doesn't tell you what readiness involves.

Start by identifying the demands of the activity: the skills, decisions, physical effort, environment and practical arrangements it requires. Your confidence is more useful when it rests on relevant preparation than on a general promise that everything will be fine.

For a climber, that might include unfamiliar movement, route reading, communicating with a partner and managing the experience of being watched. For an expedition team, it might include fatigue, changing conditions, responsibilities and decision-making when information is incomplete.

Work from the likely and important difficulties. You don't need an exhaustive catalogue of every imaginable disaster.

2Separate control from influence

Some actions are yours to organise: practise a skill, prepare equipment, clarify a role, or leave enough time for the journey.

Other things can be influenced but not commanded. You can support your recovery, but you cannot guarantee how energetic you'll feel. You can practise an attentional routine, but you cannot order anxiety to disappear.

Some conditions are outside your control: the weather, another competitor's performance, a last-minute schedule change.

The distinction matters because different problems need different responses. Trying harder won't change the weather. Accepting uncertainty won't replace a missing technical skill.

Ask: "Can I act on the situation, prepare my response, or do both?"

3Make plans specific enough to use

"I'll improve my route reading" is an intention. "During Thursday's session, I'll preview two appropriate routes, describe my plan, and compare it with what actually happens" is a practice task.

Useful planning answers what, where, when and how. It also makes room for obstacles.

Then create a few simple if-then responses. For example: "If the start is delayed, I'll check the revised timing, stay appropriately warm, and repeat a shortened version of my routine." Or: "If I make an early mistake, I'll use the next suitable pause to reset rather than change the whole plan in frustration."

These are not scripts that must be followed regardless of events. They are starting points that reduce the need to invent every response under pressure.

For activities with significant consequences, include clear limits and stop criteria. A changed plan can be evidence of good judgement, not failed commitment.

4Rehearse adaptation, then let preparation end

Knowing what you'd like to do is different from having practised it.

Bring manageable versions of relevant demands into training. Rehearse a routine with a little background noise. Practise communicating a change of plan. Ask a willing partner to observe an attempt so that being watched becomes more familiar.

Keep simulations proportionate and safe. Creating unnecessary danger or distress doesn't make preparation more realistic or effective.

Afterwards, review what happened. Did the plan help? What information did you miss? What needs simplifying?

Finally, decide when preparation is sufficient for the next step. Repeatedly checking the same plan in search of complete certainty can become another way of delaying action.

You cannot prepare for every possibility. You can arrive with relevant skills, useful options, and permission to adapt. That is a more dependable foundation than expecting yourself never to feel uncertain.
