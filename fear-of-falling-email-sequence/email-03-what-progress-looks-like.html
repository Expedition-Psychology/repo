Progress rarely feels like progress

Most climbers describe the goal as “not being scared any more”. That is usually the wrong target — and waiting for it is what keeps people stuck.

Almost everyone who comes to me describes the aim the same way: they want to stop feeling frightened.

It is an understandable goal, and it is not what getting better looks like. Holding out for it tends to keep people exactly where they are.

Fear is not a fault to be removed. On a lead wall it is frequently doing its job. What shifts with practice is not whether it turns up, but what you can do while it is there.

So what changes first

•Committing to the move instead of downclimbing to the last bolt.
•Settling within thirty seconds, rather than carrying it for the rest of the route.
•Choosing to practise falls on a session where you would previously have found a reason not to.
•Noticing your grip easing before your forearms are gone.

None of these feels like a breakthrough at the time, which is exactly why they go unnoticed. You remember the sessions that felt bad.

Making small changes visible

This is why the Toolkit asks you to rate and record what you do. An exposure ladder gives you rungs to measure against, and the logs turn a slow trend into something you can look at — which matters most on the weeks when nothing feels like it is moving.
