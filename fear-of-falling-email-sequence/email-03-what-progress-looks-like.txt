What does progress with fear of falling actually look like?
Progress is not the absence of fear. Here is what it actually tends to look like.

====================================================================
PROGRESS RARELY FEELS LIKE PROGRESS
====================================================================

Most climbers describe the goal as “not being scared any more”. That is
usually the wrong target — and waiting for it is what keeps people
stuck.

Almost everyone who comes to me describes the aim the same way: they
want to stop feeling frightened.

It is an understandable goal, and it is not what getting better looks
like. Holding out for it tends to keep people exactly where they are.

Fear is not a fault to be removed. On a lead wall it is frequently doing
its job. What shifts with practice is not whether it turns up, but what
you can do while it is there.

SO WHAT CHANGES FIRST

  - Committing to the move instead of downclimbing to the last bolt.

  - Settling within thirty seconds, rather than carrying it for the rest
    of the route.

  - Choosing to practise falls on a session where you would previously
    have found a reason not to.

  - Noticing your grip easing before your forearms are gone.

None of these feels like a breakthrough at the time, which is exactly
why they go unnoticed. You remember the sessions that felt bad.

MAKING SMALL CHANGES VISIBLE

This is why the Toolkit asks you to rate and record what you do. An
exposure ladder gives you rungs to measure against, and the logs turn a
slow trend into something you can look at — which matters most on the
weeks when nothing feels like it is moving.
